Pricing Strategy

Too high, too low, or just right? A simple five-step framework to price your work around the value you actually create.

Working out whether your prices are too high, too low, or just right is a challenge every business owner faces. But finding the right balance is more straightforward than you might think. It comes down to understanding the frameworks that drive effective pricing.

In this guide we'll explore the relationship between productisation and pricing. Productisation is about creating value at scale. Pricing is about finding the right compensation for that value. Together, they form the foundation of a successful strategy.

To be clear, pricing is far more than choosing a number. It's about clearly expressing and quantifying the value you offer. The good news is that once you've done this exercise properly, it only needs minor tweaks from there on. By the end, you'll have a framework to determine a fair price—and know when and how to adjust it.

A Five-Step Framework for Effective Pricing

Pricing well isn't a flash of inspiration; it's a process. These five steps take you from standardising what you sell, to quantifying the value it creates, to setting a number you can defend with confidence.

Step 1: Productise Your Offering

The first step toward a robust pricing strategy is productisation—standardising your services so you can scale while consistently delivering value. When your offer is well-defined, it becomes far easier to communicate what a customer is getting and, in turn, what it's worth.

A useful mental model is to split your offer into a "front-end" and a "back-end." The front-end is the visible, headline outcome your customer buys. The back-end is the repeatable system that reliably delivers it. Defining both makes your value easier to standardise, easier to explain, and easier to price.

Step 2: Estimate the Value You Provide

Understanding the value you deliver is the key to knowing how much to charge. Start by measuring the problem you solve, and be specific. How severe is the issue for your customer—a minor inconvenience or a full-blown crisis? What are the financial, operational, and emotional costs of leaving it unsolved?

Let me make this concrete. Imagine you run a legal consulting firm. Through your research you find that startups typically spend $25K a year on legal fees and 100 hours of founder time managing documents. A solution that reduces this to $10K and 10 hours would be a compelling, measurable value proposition. If the founder values her time at $120 an hour and you price at $12K, you're still saving her $13.8K a year. Depending on your circumstances, you could comfortably charge more—because you understand and can communicate the value you provide.

When value is hard to measure, look at it through four lenses: money, time, quantity, and quality. Do you help clients save money, increase revenue, save time, boost productivity, or improve the quality of what they produce? I guarantee you fall into at least one of these. A graphic designer, for example, might argue their work lifts conversion rates on marketing campaigns, which flows directly through to sales and revenue.

Finally, remember that value looks different to different stakeholders. Middle management might care about removing repetitive, inefficient work, while executives focus on the financial impact. Tailor your value proposition to each audience, and make sure your price reflects the full spectrum of value you create.

Step 3: Set Clear Pricing Goals

Your goal is to capture an "appropriate" portion of the value you provide. But what does "appropriate" actually mean? It's a balance between four things: sustainability, competitiveness, fairness, and perceived value.

Sustainability

First, is your pricing sustainable? Your rates need to maintain a healthy margin, cover your costs, and fund future growth. Price too low and you'll struggle to keep the lights on, let alone invest. Price too high and you risk driving clients away if they feel the value doesn't match the cost.

Competitiveness

Next, competitiveness. Your price signals where you sit in the market. Are you a premium service that justifies a higher number, or are you competing on affordability? Price well below the market average and you can accidentally signal lower quality; price well above it and you may alienate budget-conscious buyers. Know your position and price to it deliberately.

Fairness

Fairness matters to both sides. For you, fairness means being properly compensated for the time, effort, and expertise you bring. For your customer, it means genuinely feeling they got good value for what they paid. That sense of fairness is what builds long-term relationships and loyalty.

Think about how we navigate everyday pricing—coffee, for instance. Bean prices creep up at the supermarket, yet most of us keep buying because the value exchange still feels reasonable (especially for us Aussies 💙🇦🇺). But there's always a tipping point. Push the price too far and we start switching brands or cutting back. Your customers do exactly the same with you.

Step 4: Define Your Pricing Structure and Level

Price Structure

Your structure is how you charge—retainer, hourly, project-based, or something else. Each has trade-offs:

  • Retainer: steady, predictable revenue and easier planning, but you're committing ongoing capacity.
  • Project-based: ties your fee to a clear outcome and lets you price on value, though it offers less continuity between engagements.
  • Hourly: simple and transparent, but it quietly caps your upside and rewards you for taking longer rather than delivering faster.

Price Level

Your level is the actual number within that structure. It should reflect the value you create, your competitive position, and the strength of your brand. A well-established name with a strong reputation can command more than a new entrant offering the same thing. Make sure your level lines up with your costs, your positioning, and the value your customers perceive.

Step 5: Embrace Iteration

Pricing is not set-and-forget; it needs continuous refinement. As markets shift and client needs change, your strategy should adapt. Gather feedback, review your wins and losses, and be willing to adjust your structure or level when the evidence points that way.

The signals are usually right in front of you. If you're closing every deal with no pushback on price, you're almost certainly too cheap. If you're losing regularly on price, it's worth revisiting how well your number reflects—and communicates—your value. Invest in a sales process that supports your pricing, and stay open to experimenting with new approaches.

Common Pricing Mistakes

When you sit down to work on your pricing, avoid the pitfalls that catch most people out:

  • Failing to connect price to value (this is the big one).
  • Not understanding the competitive landscape.
  • Changing prices too quickly—or too slowly.
  • Ignoring feedback and never iterating.

Final Thoughts

I used to feel uneasy quoting my prices, especially when they were on the higher side. But after putting in the work to understand my value, I became genuinely confident in the worth of my services and the numbers I set.

Now, when a client asks about pricing, I look forward to the conversation.

I'm happy to explain my rates and share the research and case studies that show the impact of my work. With this framework, I believe anyone can get to the same place.
