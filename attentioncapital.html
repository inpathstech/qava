Attention Has Become More Valuable Than Capital

Capital is everywhere. Attention is scarce. What the unlikely rise of Anti Fund reveals about where business is heading — and why your audience is becoming the real moat.

By Qava Team

There was a time when the most important thing in business was access to capital.

If you had an idea, ambition, and the ability to execute, your biggest challenge was convincing someone to fund it. Venture capitalists controlled the gates. Banks controlled the gates. Investors controlled the gates. Money was scarce, and scarcity creates power.

Today, that world feels increasingly distant.

Capital Is No Longer the Bottleneck

Capital is everywhere.

The largest venture capital firms are sitting on mountains of cash. Angel investors can deploy capital from their phones. Family offices, sovereign wealth funds, private equity firms, hedge funds, and corporate venture groups are all searching for opportunities. If you are a talented founder building something genuinely interesting, there is no shortage of people willing to write a cheque.

The bottleneck has moved.

What is scarce now is attention.

The Most Unlikely Story in Venture Capital

That is why one of the most fascinating stories in venture capital over the past few years isn't about a legendary Silicon Valley investor or a Stanford professor. It is about Jake Paul.

On the surface, it makes no sense.

Jake Paul became famous making videos on the internet. He built an audience through entertainment, controversy, and an uncanny understanding of how to capture attention. Millions followed him. Millions criticised him. But almost nobody would have predicted that the same person would go on to build one of the most enviable venture portfolios in technology.

Yet today, Anti Fund has exposure to companies that most investors would do almost anything to access. OpenAI. SpaceX. Anduril. Ramp. Cognition.

The question is not whether Jake Paul is a good investor.

The question is how someone who built his reputation in entertainment gained access to opportunities that many of the world's most sophisticated investors never see.

The answer reveals something important about where business is heading.

Two Forms of Leverage

Most people tell the Anti Fund story incorrectly because they assume it is a celebrity story.

It isn't.

It is a story about leverage.

More specifically, it is a story about combining two forms of leverage that are becoming increasingly powerful in the modern economy.

The first is technical capability.

The second is audience.

On one side of Anti Fund sits Geoffrey Woo, a Stanford-trained computer scientist, founder, operator, and investor. Before Anti Fund, Woo had already built and sold companies, earned credibility in technical circles, and developed a reputation for understanding products, engineering, and venture investing at a deep level.

On the other side sits Jake Paul.

Where Geoffrey understands technology, Jake understands attention.
Where Geoffrey understands product, Jake understands distribution.
Where Geoffrey understands how to build, Jake understands how to make people care.

Separately, each is valuable.

Together, they represent something increasingly difficult to compete against.

Why Technology Alone Has Never Been Enough

For decades, Silicon Valley worshipped technical capability above all else. The best founders were engineers. The strongest companies had the best products. The assumption was that superior technology would eventually win.

There is truth in that.

Technology matters.
Product matters.
Execution matters.

But technology alone has never been enough.

History is littered with examples of superior products losing to superior stories.

The reality is that the market does not reward what is best. The market rewards what is understood.

A breakthrough innovation that nobody notices has little value.

A founder who cannot communicate their vision struggles to attract customers, talent, media attention, and investors.

The best technology in the world cannot create momentum if nobody understands why it matters.

Attention Is Trust at Scale

This is where attention becomes so powerful.

Most people think of attention as vanity. Followers. Likes. Views. Subscribers.

They mistake the scorecard for the asset.

The real asset is trust.

Attention is simply trust at scale.

When people trust you, they listen. When they listen, you gain distribution. When you have distribution, you can launch products faster, recruit better people, raise capital more efficiently, and recover more quickly when things go wrong.

An audience is not merely a collection of followers.

It is a distribution network that belongs to you.

And unlike capital, which can be copied, distribution is surprisingly difficult to replicate.

This is why some venture capital firms have begun recruiting creators, writers, podcasters, and audience builders. It is why founders spend more time developing personal brands. It is why newsletters, communities, podcasts, YouTube channels, and social media followings have become strategic assets rather than marketing side projects.

The smartest people in technology have realised something that much of corporate America has not.

The world is drowning in products.

What it lacks is attention.

Narrative Is Strategy

Every day thousands of startups launch. Many are technically impressive. Many are solving meaningful problems. Many are built by talented teams.

Most will never be noticed.

Not because they are bad.

Because nobody is paying attention.

In that environment, narrative becomes a competitive advantage.

Narrative helps people understand what is changing.
Narrative helps people understand why now matters.
Narrative helps people understand why they should care.

Founders often dismiss storytelling as something soft. Something secondary. Something that belongs to marketing departments.

That is a mistake.

Storytelling is not decoration. It is strategy.

The ability to clearly articulate a vision may be one of the most important skills an entrepreneur can develop.

Because the truth is that customers do not buy products.

They buy stories about themselves.

Employees do not join companies.
They join missions.

Investors do not fund spreadsheets.
They fund narratives about the future.

The best founders understand this instinctively.

They know that technology creates possibility, but narrative creates belief.

And belief changes behaviour.

The Real Lesson from Anti Fund

That may be the most important lesson from Anti Fund.

Not that a YouTuber became a venture capitalist.

Not that a boxer built a portfolio that includes some of the most sought-after companies in the world.

The real lesson is that the economy is changing.

The old world rewarded those who controlled capital.

The new world increasingly rewards those who control attention.

The winners of the next decade will not necessarily be the people with the most money.

Nor will they necessarily be the people with the best technology.

They will be the people who can combine capability with distribution.

People who can build something meaningful and then convince the world that it matters.

Because in an age where capital is abundant and technology is increasingly accessible, attention has become the scarce resource.

And scarcity creates power.

Your audience is no longer a by-product of success. Increasingly, it is the moat itself.

Build something worth paying attention to.

In an economy where capital is abundant, distribution is the advantage.
