Cab Driver vs. Changemaker

When you show up for a client, a company, or a problem — are you showing up as a cab driver or a changemaker? It sounds too simple to matter. It quietly separates the people who stay "reliable" from the ones who become indispensable.

The Question Behind the Question

Every so often a single question reorganises how you see your own work, and this is one of them: when you show up — for a client, for a company, for a problem — are you showing up as a cab driver or a changemaker?

It sounds almost too simple to matter. But the more I've sat with it, the more I've realised it quietly separates the people who stay stuck at "reliable" from the people who become genuinely indispensable. It's not a question about talent or hustle. It's a question about the posture you take toward the work.

What a Cab Driver Actually Does

Picture the cab driver, and be fair to them, because there's nothing wrong with the job. They get signalled. They're told where to go. The meter starts running, and off they go. They deliver the passenger to the destination the passenger named, they get paid, and they do it again with the next fare.

It's honest work and it's competent work. But notice the shape of it: the driver never chooses the destination. They never ask whether it's the right destination. They execute a route someone else defined, and their responsibility begins and ends at the curb. It's clean, it's transactional, and it's entirely reactive. The passenger has to already know where they're going for the driver to be useful at all.

That's the trap, though — because so much of professional life is structured to reward exactly this. You get handed a brief. You execute the brief. You get paid. Everyone's satisfied, nothing goes wrong, and you never once stopped to ask whether the destination the client named was actually where they needed to end up.

You were excellent at the route. You just never questioned the map.

What a Changemaker Does Instead

A changemaker takes the wheel completely differently. They don't sit at the curb waiting to be told where to go. They show the client where they need to go, why they need to go there, and how to get there faster and more efficiently than the client could have figured out on their own.

That's the whole shift, right there. The cab driver answers the question they were asked. The changemaker interrogates whether it was the right question in the first place — and then delivers a better one along with the answer.

This is the part that makes a changemaker worth so much more than a competent executor. They take ownership of the problem and the solution. Not just their slice of it, not just the leg of the route they were assigned, but the whole arc from "here's what you think you want" to "here's what will actually move you forward."

A changemaker helps a client achieve more than they could ever have achieved alone — not because the changemaker is smarter, but because they refuse to treat the client's initial ask as the ceiling. They treat it as the starting line.

Why This Hits Different When You Work for Yourself

This distinction is everything when you're starting or growing a company, and if you've ever made the jump from employee to entrepreneur, you've felt it in your body whether or not you had words for it. Inside a company, you can get away with being a very good cab driver. There's a structure above you defining the destinations. There's someone whose job it is to ask "why" so that yours can be "how." You can be reactive and still be rewarded, because the system absorbs the strategic thinking on your behalf.

The moment you work for yourself, that scaffolding vanishes. There's no one handing you the destination anymore. Nobody signals you. The meter doesn't start unless you decide where the trip is going and convince someone it's worth taking.

And here's the humbling part: even if you always thought of yourself as a changemaker in your last job — even if you were genuinely the most proactive person on your team — working for yourself pushes you to a level you didn't know existed. The bar moves. It stops being about meeting expectations, because now you're the one setting them. It becomes about exceeding a standard that only you are holding yourself to.

Ownership Is the Whole Game

What it really comes down to is ownership, and ownership is uncomfortable precisely because it can't be handed to you. A cab driver's responsibility is bounded — deliver the fare, collect the payment, done. A changemaker's responsibility is unbounded by design. They own the outcome, which means they own the ambiguity, the risk of pointing at the wrong destination, and the work of getting a client to trust a route they didn't ask for.

You can't be indispensable and comfortable at the same time.

That discomfort is the tax you pay for being worth more. Indispensability comes from taking on exactly the parts other people would rather leave at the curb.

The Lesson

So the lesson is simple to say and hard to live: be a changemaker. Don't wait to be signalled. Don't just run the route you were handed. Show people where they actually need to go, explain why, get them there faster than they could have alone, and take ownership of the whole thing — problem and solution both.

The cab is always the safer business. The changemaker is the one who gets to decide where the industry drives next.

Be the changemaker.

The best work comes from people who own the problem and the solution.
